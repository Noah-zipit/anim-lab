    The unglamorous essentials.

    Why springs feel alive

    A keyframed animation follows a script; a spring follows physics. Give a spring a target and it negotiates — overshooting when it carries speed, settling when damping takes the energy out. That negotiation is why spring-driven interfaces feel responsive instead of choreographed: interrupt one, and it simply changes its mind mid-flight, velocity intact.

    Stiffness120 – 260
    Damping ratio0.5 – 0.8
    Settle time≈ 600 ms
    Stiffness is the spring constant: how aggressively the spring pulls its object toward the target. Raise it and everything snaps faster and overshoots harder. Most UI springs live between 120 and 260 — below that, interfaces feel drunk; above it, they feel rigid.
    Damping bleeds energy out of the system on every frame. A damping ratio under 1 means the spring oscillates — that is the bounce. Around 0.6 you get one friendly overshoot; at 1.0 the motion lands dead on target with no bounce at all.
    Mass decides how much the object resists being moved. Heavy elements start slower and stop later, which is why a big card should never use the same spring as a tiny badge. Match the spring to the visual weight and the motion reads as physical.
    Springs remember

    A spring carries its velocity through an interruption, so a reversed animation never visibly restarts. Motion stays continuous because the physics never stopped.

    Easing is meaning

    An ease-out says "arriving", an ease-in says "leaving", and an overshoot says "playful". The curve is read before the content is — choose it like a word.

    Physics over keyframes

    Keyframes break the moment a user interrupts them. Springs do not have a timeline to break — only a target, a velocity, and a rule for losing energy.
